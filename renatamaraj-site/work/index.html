Migrating Hermes: PayPal's 15-year-old checkout system

10 months · cross-org migration

Hermes was PayPal's original checkout application — roughly 15 years old, and still carrying about 3% of live checkout traffic when it landed on my team. Several managers before me had tried and failed to retire it. It was a monolith with stakeholders spread across billing and subscriptions, guest checkout, and the SDK team, and my org was the one uniquely on the hook for its reliability, its security patching, and its maintenance — a growing burden as fewer engineers across the company still knew Angular. Worse, its existence was actively blocking new products like Pay in 4 from shipping.

I started by instrumenting the app to isolate exactly how much of the remaining traffic was ours, and built a shared dashboard so every stakeholder team could see the same numbers. That dashboard became the tool I used to get my director to align the other teams' leadership, and to scope the work into epics per team. From there it was endpoint-by-endpoint migration, working directly with the other teams' engineering managers and PMs rather than waiting for a mandate from above.

After 10 months, our share of traffic on Hermes dropped from 3% to 0.1%, and we fully handed the app off to the billing and subscriptions team — the one org that still had real usage of it — taking the release and maintenance burden off a team that had been carrying it for years longer than it should have.

Rebuilding onboarding from the ground up

adopted by two additional orgs

Onboarding was in bad shape when I took over the team: contractors weren't reliably getting laptops on time, and during a period of scaled hiring, new engineers could go days without a manager to answer to. I rebuilt it from the ground up — a round-robin onboarding-buddy system, correct provisioning tracked end to end, and a structured 30/60/90-day plan that gave every new hire the same runway: compliance in week one, company-wide onboarding in week two, org context in week three, and hands-on work with our actual applications and release process by week four.

New hires got 1:1s with every team member and formal introductions to cross-functional partners. I ran a live onboarding session every month in addition to the recordings, so people could ask questions in real time instead of guessing. And I set an explicit expectation: a completed pull request within the first 30 days, even if it was just a content fix — because momentum in the first month matters more than most managers give it credit for.

Within about three weeks, new engineers were contributing meaningfully instead of still finding their footing. The buddy program was later adopted, unprompted, by both the Compliance org and the Developer Productivity org.
